\begin{abstract}
For a residue $r$ modulo $2^N$, let $R_N(r)$ be the least height
$\max(|a|,b)$ of a rational representation $a\equiv rb\pmod{2^N}$
with positive odd denominator $b$.
We prove that the uniform mean of the ordinary, unrounded quantity
$\log_2R_N(r)$ is at most $N/2$ for every $N\ge1$.
This is the mean part of Question~3.1 in the 2026 survey of Lin, Xiao and Chen.
The proof compares the product of the two lift heights with a truncated
logarithmic excess at the parent modulus. Short lattice vectors with even
denominator cover the large-height residues; completion in the actual odd
coset bounds their contribution. A finite lattice sum, a two-prime sieve
and an explicit primitive-point estimate close the inequality for parent
length at least $26$. Integer certificates cover the remaining parent
lengths $1,\ldots,25$. We also derive the complete finite distribution,
hence an exact finite arithmetic-logarithmic formula for the logarithmic
variance. A marked-lattice limit identifies the normalized height with
a published Boca--Gologan law and yields the limiting logarithmic
variance $0.892302571204274625294755\ldots$. The raw-height mean and
limiting Jensen gap are evaluated. A batched histogram algorithm uses
$O(N2^N)$ arithmetic operations and $O(2^N)$ integer storage slots.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the results}
Write a binary string as $s=(s_0,\ldots,s_{N-1})$, and identify it with
$r=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}s_i2^i$ modulo $2^N$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:def}
 R_N(r)=\min_{\substack{a\equiv rb\pmod{2^N}\\b>0,\ b\ \odd}}
                   \max(|a|,b),\qquad
 \mathcal E_N=2^{-N}\sum_{r=0}^{2^N-1}\log_2R_N(r).
\end{equation}
This is the rational height underlying ordinary finite $2$-adic complexity.
The logarithm is not rounded to an integer. The measure includes every
string, including the zero string; it is not a measure on odd residues.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every integer $N\ge1$,
\[
                         \mathcal E_N\le \frac N2.
\]
\end{theorem}

Tian and Qi studied this same mean and proved a weaker upper bound
\cite[Theorem~3]{TQ}. Chen and Winterhof proved the lower bound
$\mathcal E_N\ge N/2-1$ and an upper bound with a logarithmic error
\cite[Theorems~2--3]{CWv1}. Their short primitive counts credit Tian--Qi;
their growth recurrence credits Goresky--Klapper \cite{GK}.
The exact upper in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is explicitly asked for in
\cite[Question~3.1]{LXC}, together with a separate variance problem.
We give the exact mean bound, a finite formula for the logarithmic
variance, and its evaluated limit. The finite formula is a bounded
arithmetic sum involving logarithms, not a short elementary expression
in $N$.

The exact-mean proof uses elementary geometry of congruence lattices and finite
integer certificates. It does not use a limiting distribution to infer
an inequality at every length. Its computational premises are stated in
Section~\ref{sec:finite} and checked by the accompanying dependency-free
program. The uniform argument is a proof for all sufficiently large
lengths, not an extrapolation of those certificates.

Sections~\ref{sec:law}--\ref{sec:algorithm} give the complete finite
distribution, moment sums and a batched histogram algorithm. For
$Z_N=R_N/2^{N/2}$, Sections~\ref{sec:bridge}--\ref{sec:BG} prove a
marked-lattice limit and identify its survival as $G_2(t^2)$, where
$G_2$ is the published Boca--Gologan law. Section~\ref{sec:moments}
evaluates its logarithmic moments and transfers them to binary strings:
\[
\mathcal E_N=N/2+\frac{7\zeta(3)/(2\pi^2)-1}{2\ln2}+o(1),
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\log_2R_N)\longrightarrow
0.892302571204274625294755\ldots.
\]
Section~\ref{sec:raw} evaluates the leading raw-mean coefficient and
the positive limiting Jensen gap. The law itself is known; the dyadic
identification, finite formulas and moment evaluations are stated
separately, with their external hypotheses at the point of use.

\paragraph{Notation.}
Logarithms without a displayed base are natural; finite-string moments use base two. The subscript in $R_N$ denotes
a length. When a parent modulus is $M=2^n$, abbreviate $R_M(r)=R_n(r)$;
thus $R_{2M}$ denotes the child height $R_{n+1}$. Put
\[
 q=\sqrt M,\qquad s=q/\sqrt2,\qquad J=\lfloor n/2\rfloor.
\]
The convention $[x]_+=\max(x,0)$ will be used.
A minimum in \eqref{eq:def} is primitive in $\Z^2$: its common divisor
is odd and invertible modulo $2^N$, so division would otherwise lower
its height. In particular $R_M(0)=1$ and $R_M(r)\le M/2$ for $M\ge2$.

\section{The two lifts and a logarithmic budget}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:children}
Let $M=2^n$, $n\ge1$. For a residue $r$ modulo $M$, put
\[
 h=R_M(r),\qquad H=\max\{R_{2M}(r),R_{2M}(r+M)\}.
\]
Then $\min\{R_{2M}(r),R_{2M}(r+M)\}=h$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:product}
                            hH\le\max(M,2h^2).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The union of the two sets of child representations is exactly the set of
parent representations, since an odd denominator is invertible modulo
$2M$. Choose a primitive parent minimum $u=(a,b)$, $b>0$ odd.
It selects a child $r_0$ with height $h$.
Reflection allows $a\ge0$.

Suppose first $a>0$. Integer solutions $v=(c,d)$ of
$ad-bc=M$ form $v_0+ku$ by B\'ezout's identity. The restriction $d$ odd
selects exactly one class of $k$ modulo $2$. Moreover
\[
 c\equiv(r_0+M)d\pmod{2M}.
\]
Indeed $a\equiv r_0b\pmod{2M}$ and $b^{-1}M\equiv M\pmod{2M}$,
while $Md\equiv M$ for odd $d$. A negative $d$ can be oriented positive
by negating $v$; its height and child are unchanged.

Put $\tau=\min(a,b)$, $h=\max(a,b)$, $\sigma=a+b$ and
$X=\max\{h,(M+2ab)/\sigma\}$.
Parameterize the real determinant line by
$(-M/\sigma,M/\sigma)+t(a,b)$.
If $M\le h(h-\tau)$, then $X=h$. When $a=h$, the full interval
$c\in[-h,h]$ gives $|d|=|(M+bc)/a|\le h$; its length in $t$ is $2$.
When $b=h$, use the full interval $d\in[-h,h]$ instead.
If $M\ge h(h-\tau)$, then $X=(M+2ab)/\sigma\ge h$. The points at
$t=-2b/\sigma$ and $t=2a/\sigma$ are
\[
 \left(-X,\frac{M-2b^2}{\sigma}\right),\qquad
 \left(\frac{-M+2a^2}{\sigma},X\right).
\]
Both belong to $[-X,X]^2$: the required conditions are
$M\ge b(b-a)$ and $M\ge a(a-b)$, which follow from the case assumption.
The closed segment between them has parameter length $2$.
In either case it intersects the class $t_0+2\Z$.
Thus the other child has height at most $X$.

Writing $x=\tau/h$, we obtain
\[
 hH\le\max\left\{h^2,\frac{M+2h^2x}{1+x}\right\}
       \le\max(M,2h^2).
\]
If $a=0$, primitivity forces $b=1$, the children are $0$ and $M$, and
their heights are $1$ and $M$, so the same conclusion holds.
Ties between parent minima need no special choice.
\end{proof}

Define the exact finite quantities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budgets}
\begin{split}
 C_M(t)&=\#\{r\bmod M:R_M(r)\le t\},\\
 K_M&=\frac1M\sum_r\pos{\ln(R_M(r)/s)},\\
 \mathcal D_M&=\frac1M\int_s^q C_M(t)\,\frac{dt}{t},\\
 \mathcal L_M&=\frac1M\sum_r\pos{\ln(R_M(r)/q)}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
We reserve $D$ without a calligraphic letter for a dyadic content below.
Taking logarithms in \eqref{eq:product}, and averaging over both children,
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduction}
 \E_{r\bmod2M}\ln R_{2M}(r)\le \frac12\ln M+K_M.
\end{equation}
There is no induction hypothesis on the parent mean.

For every $R>0$,
\[
 \pos{\ln(R/s)}
 =\ln(q/s)-\int_s^q {\bf1}_{R\le t}\frac{dt}{t}
             +\pos{\ln(R/q)}.
\]
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:identity}
 K_M=\frac12\ln2-\mathcal D_M+\mathcal L_M.
\end{equation}
It suffices to prove $K_M\le\frac12\ln2$ at every parent length.
For large lengths we prove $\mathcal L_M<\mathcal D_M$.

\section{Short even vectors and the actual odd coset}\label{sec:cusps}
For $r\bmod M$ let
\[
 \Lambda_r=\{(A,B)\in\Z^2:A-rB\in M\Z\}.
\]
Its basis $(M,0),(r,1)$ has determinant $M$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:certificate}
If $R_M(r)>q$, there exist
\[
 D=2^j,\quad1\le j\le J,\quad b>0\ \odd,\quad
 \gcd(a,b)=1,\quad D\max(|a|,b)\le q
\]
such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contact}
           a-rb=(M/D)\alpha_r,\qquad \alpha_r\ \odd.
\end{equation}
For each such triple, its cell $\mathcal C(D,a,b)$ of residues satisfying
\eqref{eq:contact} has exactly $D/2$ elements.
For each residue in that cell, $D(a,b)$ is primitive as an element of
$\Lambda_r$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Minkowski's convex body theorem \cite{Cassels} in the square of radius
$q+\varepsilon$ gives a nonzero lattice vector of height at most
$q+\varepsilon$. Discreteness permits passage to the closed radius $q$.
Choose a shortest nonzero vector. Its denominator is nonzero, since a
nonzero horizontal vector has height at least $M>q$.
Orient it positive. The hypothesis $R_M(r)>q$ forces its denominator
even, and its numerator is then even as well.
A common odd factor could be divided out while preserving lattice
membership, contradicting minimality. Thus its content is $D=2^j\ge2$;
write it as $D(a,b)$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$.
The congruence modulo $Q=M/D$, which is even, forces $b$ odd.
If $\alpha_r=(a-rb)/Q$ were even, division by $2$ would again yield
a shorter vector of $\Lambda_r$. This proves the asserted contact.

There is one solution of $a-rb\equiv0\pmod Q$. Its $D$ lifts modulo $M$
change $\alpha_r$ by $-bt$, $t\bmod D$. Exactly $D/2$ have odd contact.
The coordinates of $D(a,b)$ in the displayed lattice basis are
$(\alpha_r,Db)$. Their gcd is $1$: $\alpha_r$ is odd, and
$\gcd(\alpha_r,b)=1$ follows from $\gcd(a,b)=1$ and $\gcd(Q,b)=1$.
This is lattice primitivity, despite the even integer content.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:completion}
On a cell $\mathcal C(D,a,b)$ put $A=D|a|$, $B=Db$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:X}
 R_M(r)\le X:=\frac{M+AB}{A+B},\qquad X\ge q.
\end{equation}
On the axis $a=0,b=1$, the height is exactly $R_M(r)=M/D$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $a>0$, lattice primitivity allows completion of $v=(A,B)$ to a
basis with determinant $M$. Such completions $w=(c,d)$ satisfy
\[
 ad-bc=Q,\qquad ad\equiv Q\pmod b,\qquad
                       \alpha_r d\equiv1\pmod D.
\]
The last two congruences give one class of odd $d$ modulo $B=Db$ by
the Chinese remainder theorem. Invert $a$ only modulo $b$; no inversion
modulo an even denominator is needed. For $b=1$ the first congruence
is empty. All completions differ by a multiple of $v$.

Put $x=M/(A+B)$. Let $d_-\le x<d_+=d_-+B$ be the adjacent denominators
in this class, and write $\eta=x-d_-\in[0,B)$.
On the determinant line, $c(d)=(Ad-M)/B$. The two heights are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adjacent}
 \max(|c(d_-)|,|d_-|)=x+(A/B)\eta,\qquad
 \max(|c(d_+)|,|d_+|)=x+B-\eta.
\end{equation}
For clarity, the sign checks use $A,B\le q$.
If $d_-<0$ and $A<B$, then
$(B-A)|d_-|\le(B-A)(B-x)\le B^2\le M$, so $-c(d_-)\ge|d_-|$;
the case $A\ge B$ is immediate.
At $d_+>x$, $-c(d_+)\le d_+$.
If $A>B$, then $c(d_+)\le d_+$ follows from
$(A-B)(x+B)\le M$, equivalent to $A^2-B^2\le2M$.
The other sign cases follow directly from $d_-\le x<d_+$.
The smaller expression in \eqref{eq:adjacent} is at most
$x+AB/(A+B)=X$, since the two affine expressions meet at
$\eta=B^2/(A+B)$.
Negative odd denominators are oriented positive by negating both
coordinates. Reflection handles $a<0$.
Finally $X/q\ge1$ is equivalent to
$(1-A/q)(1-B/q)\ge0$.

If $a=0$, primitivity gives $b=1$ and $r=-Q\alpha_r$.
Every marked numerator is a nonzero odd multiple of $Q$, so the height
is at least $Q$.
Take $c=-Q$ and a positive odd $d\le D-1$ representing
$\alpha_r^{-1}\pmod D$. This has height $Q$, because $D\le q\le Q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Only coverage and the cell masses are needed for our upper bound.
We do not require disjointness of the cells or replace their actual
odd phases by continuous phase averages. The union bound below remains
valid even without a uniqueness statement.
\end{remark}

Let $V=\{r\ne0:v_2(r)\ge\lceil n/2\rceil\}$.
The axis cells are precisely these residues: for $D=2^j$ their valuation
is $k=n-j$ and their height is $2^k$.
This family is already present in Tian--Qi \cite[Lemma~4]{TQ}.
Write $\mathcal L_V$ for its contribution to $\mathcal L_M$ and
$\mathcal L_{\rm other}=\mathcal L_M-\mathcal L_V$.
For $0<x,y\le1$ define
\[
 f(x,y)=\ln\frac{1+xy}{x+y},\qquad
 P_f(T)=\sum_{\substack{1\le a,b\le T\\b\ \odd,\ \gcd(a,b)=1}}
                                           f(a/T,b/T).
\]
The kernel is nonnegative. Every non-axis residue with height above $q$
is covered by Lemma~\ref{lem:certificate}, and Lemma~\ref{lem:completion}
bounds its energy by $f(D|a|/q,Db/q)$.
Each cell has $D/2$ residues and both signs of $a$ give the weight $D$.
Therefore, using an upper bound by a union of cells,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U}
 \mathcal L_{\rm other}\le U_M:=\frac1M
                         \sum_{j=1}^J2^jP_f(q/2^j).
\end{equation}
Including cells whose height is at most $q$ only enlarges this upper
bound; all summands are nonnegative.

\section{An explicit estimate of the lattice sum}
Let
\[
 I=\int_{[0,1]^2}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy,\qquad
 F(T)=\sum_{\substack{1\le a,b\le T\\b\ \odd}}f(a/T,b/T).
\]
The integral is finite: $0\le f(x,y)\le-\ln\max(x,y)$, so $I\le1/2$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:grid}
For every real $T>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grid}
                   \frac I2T^2-T\le F(T)\le\frac I2T^2,
 \qquad I=\frac{\zeta(2)-1}{2}=\frac{\pi^2}{12}-\frac12.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For fixed $0<x\le1$, extend $f(x,y)$ to $y\ge0$. Its derivatives satisfy
\[
 \partial_yf=\frac{x^2-1}{(1+xy)(x+y)}\le0,\qquad
 \partial_y^2f=\frac1{(x+y)^2}-\frac{x^2}{(1+xy)^2}\ge0.
\]
The last assertion follows from $1+xy\ge x(x+y)$, also beyond $y=1$.
Odd $b\le T$ are the midpoints of intervals
$[(b-1)/T,(b+1)/T]$. Convexity gives
\[
 \frac2T\sum_{b\le T,\ b\ \odd} f(x,b/T)
 \le\int_0^{Y_T}f(x,y)\,dy,\qquad
 Y_T=\frac{2\lceil\lfloor T\rfloor/2\rceil}{T}.
\]
If $Y_T\le1$, the omitted portion is nonnegative.
If $Y_T>1$, the added portion is nonpositive, because $f(x,y)\le0$
for $y>1$. Thus this integral is at most
$\Phi(x)=\int_0^1f(x,y)\,dy$.
The function $\Phi$ is decreasing and nonnegative, so its right
Riemann sum at $a/T$, $1\le a\le\lfloor T\rfloor$, is at most $TI$.
This proves the upper bound for real $T$, including the last odd cell.

For $T\ge1$, the clipped rectangles
$[a/T,(a+1)/T]\times[b/T,(b+2)/T]$ with the same indices cover
$[1/T,1]^2$ with disjoint interiors. Monotonicity bounds the integral
there by $2F(T)/T^2$.
The omitted vertical and horizontal strips have total integral at most
$2/T$, using $f\le-\ln y$ and $f\le-\ln x$, respectively.
This proves the lower bound. For $0<T<1$, $F(T)=0$ and the lower bound
is nonpositive.

For $z\ge1$, the area of the region $(1+xy)/(x+y)\ge z$ is
\[
 \mathcal A(z)=\int_0^{1/z}\frac{1-zx}{z-x}\,dx
 =1-(z^2-1)\ln\frac{z^2}{z^2-1}
 =\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{z^{-2k}}{k(k+1)}.
\]
The endpoint $z=1$ is interpreted by a limit.
Layer cake and Tonelli's theorem give
\[
 I=\int_1^\infty\mathcal A(z)\,\frac{dz}{z}
   =\frac12\sum_{k\ge1}\frac1{k^2(k+1)}
   =\frac{\zeta(2)-1}{2}.
\]
The last expression uses
$1/(k^2(k+1))=1/k^2-1/k+1/(k+1)$ and Euler's classical
evaluation $\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$.
\end{proof}

A primitive pair cannot have either $3$ or $5$ as a common divisor.
Since $f\ge0$, inclusion--exclusion in the larger set which excludes
only those two common divisors gives
\[
 P_f(T)\le F(T)-F(T/3)-F(T/5)+F(T/15).
\]
Dividing by an odd common divisor preserves the odd denominator.
Using the upper bound in \eqref{eq:grid} in the positive terms and
the lower bound in the negative terms yields
\[
 P_f(T)\le\frac{32I}{75}T^2+\frac8{15}T.
\]
This includes scaled radii smaller than $1$.
Substitution in \eqref{eq:U} gives the uniform finite estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail}
 \mathcal L_{\rm other}\le
 \frac{32I}{75}(1-2^{-J})+\frac{8J}{15q}.
\end{equation}

\section{Central credit and the infinite range}
For integer $T\ge1$, define the signed primitive count, with the axis once,
\[
 A(T)=\#\{(a,b):|a|\le T,\ 1\le b\le T,\ b\ \odd,\ \gcd(a,b)=1\}.
\]
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:count}
For every integer $T\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:count}
 A(T)\ge\frac8{\pi^2}T^2-\frac34T\ln T-\frac{11}4T-\frac14.
\end{equation}
For $n\ge2$, $T_0=\lfloor q/\sqrt2\rfloor$ satisfies
$C_M(T_0)=A(T_0)$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:central}
 \mathcal D_M\ge\frac{2\ln2}{\pi^2}
 -\frac{4\sqrt2\ln2/\pi^2+
       (\ln2/(2\sqrt2))((3/4)\ln q+11/4)}q
 -\frac{\ln2}{8q^2}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
M\"obius inversion over odd common divisors gives the exact formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mobius}
 A(T)=\sum_{\substack{g\le T\\g\ \odd}}
       \mu(g)(2\lfloor T/g\rfloor+1)
                   \left\lceil\frac{\lfloor T/g\rfloor}{2}\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
For $l=\lfloor x\rfloor$ the raw count
$B(l)=(2l+1)\lceil l/2\rceil$ equals $l^2+l/2$ when $l$ is even and
$l^2+3l/2+1/2$ when it is odd. Hence
$|B(l)-x^2|\le3x/2+1/2$.
Also
\[
 \sum_{\substack{g\le T\\g\ \odd}}\frac1g\le1+\frac12\ln T,
 \qquad \#\{g\le T:g\ \odd\}\le(T+1)/2.
\]
For the first inequality,
$1/(2k+1)\le\frac12\ln((2k+1)/(2k-1))$ for $k\ge1$ telescopes.
The raw error in \eqref{eq:mobius} is at most
$(3/4)T\ln T+(7/4)T+1/4$.
The absolutely convergent Euler product gives
$\sum_{g\ \odd}\mu(g)/g^2=8/\pi^2$; truncating it at $T$ costs at most
$T^2\sum_{g>T}g^{-2}\le T$.
This proves \eqref{eq:count}.

Two distinct primitive representations in the $T_0$ box of one residue
have a nonzero determinant divisible by $M$, unless they coincide.
The determinant has absolute value at most $2T_0B_0$, where $B_0$
is the largest odd integer at most $T_0$.
If $2T_0^2<M$, this is less than $M$.
If $2T_0^2=M$ and $n\ge2$, $T_0$ is a power of $2$ and is even, so
$B_0=T_0-1$ and the strict inequality still holds.
A zero determinant gives the same primitive fraction with positive
denominator. Thus the residue map is injective; every covered residue
has a primitive representation in the box, so $C_M(T_0)=A(T_0)$.
The excluded case $n=1$ has the collision $(1,1),(-1,1)$.

By monotonicity $\mathcal D_M\ge(\ln2)/(2M)A(T_0)$.
Use $T_0^2\ge q^2/2-\sqrt2q$,
$T_0\ln T_0\le(q/\sqrt2)\ln q$ and $T_0\le q/\sqrt2$ in
\eqref{eq:count}. This yields \eqref{eq:central}.
\end{proof}

The axis mass at valuation $k$ is $2^{-k-1}$. Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:axis}
 \frac{\mathcal L_V}{\ln2}
 =\sum_{k=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n-1}2^{-k-1}(k-n/2)
 =
 \begin{cases}
 2^{-\ell}-(\ell+1)2^{-2\ell},&n=2\ell,\ \ell\ge1,\\
 3\,2^{-\ell-2}-(2\ell+3)2^{-2\ell-2},&n=2\ell+1,\ \ell\ge0.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In particular $\mathcal L_V\le\sqrt2\ln2/q$ for all $n\ge1$.
These are finite geometric sums, including the zero energies at $n=1,2$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cutoff}
For every parent length $n\ge26$,
$\mathcal L_M<\mathcal D_M$, hence $K_M<\frac12\ln2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put
\[
 c=\frac{32I}{75}=\frac{16}{75}\left(\frac{\pi^2}{6}-1\right),
 \qquad \delta=\frac{2\ln2}{\pi^2}-c.
\]
Equations \eqref{eq:tail}, \eqref{eq:central}, \eqref{eq:axis}, discarding
the favorable term $-c2^{-J}$, give
$\mathcal D_M-\mathcal L_M\ge\delta-E(q,J)$, where
\[
 E(q,J)=\frac{8J/15+4\sqrt2\ln2/\pi^2+
       (\ln2/(2\sqrt2))((3/4)\ln q+11/4)+\sqrt2\ln2}{q}
                   +\frac{\ln2}{8q^2}.
\]
Use $3<\pi<22/7$, $1<\sqrt2<3/2$, and
$\ln2>2/3+2/81=56/81$ from the positive atanh series. Then
\[
 \delta>\frac{1372}{9801}-\frac{304}{2205}
        =\frac{45756}{21611205}>\frac1{500}.
\]
Since $q\ge2^J$ and $\ln q<J+1$, the same elementary bounds give
\[
 E(q,J)\le
 \left(\frac{109}{120}J+\frac{47}{12}\right)2^{-J}
                   +\frac18\,2^{-2J}.
\]
At $J=13$ the right side is
$629/327680+1/536870912<1/520+1/13000=1/500$.
Both terms decrease for $J\ge13$; for the first,
$aJ+b>a$ with $a=109/120$, $b=47/12$.
Thus $n\ge26$ implies $E<1/500<\delta$.
\end{proof}

\section{Finite certificates and completion of the proof}\label{sec:finite}
The remaining premise is $K_{2^n}\le\frac12\ln2$ for $1\le n\le25$.
There are two different finite interfaces, described here so the computation
has an exact mathematical meaning. No decision uses floating logarithms.

For $n=1,\ldots,12$, certificates give the height $h_r$ and a primitive
witness $(a_r,b_r)$ for every residue. The checker verifies
$b_r>0$ odd, $a_r\equiv rb_r$, and
$\max(|a_r|,b_r)=h_r$. To prove minimality, for every positive odd $b<h_r$
it computes the least absolute representative of $rb\bmod M$ and
checks that it is at least $h_r$. This excludes every improving pair.
Let $c_n=\#\{r:2h_r^2>M\}$ and
$P_n=\prod_{2h_r^2>M}h_r^2$. Then the required comparison is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Kinteger}
                P_n\le 2^{\,M+(n-1)c_n}.
\end{equation}
This follows by exponentiating $2MK_M\le M\ln2$.
The full domain has $\sum_{n=1}^{12}2^n=8190$ minima.
For diagnostic support, the checker also verifies
$\prod_r h_r^2\le M^M$, but \eqref{eq:Kinteger} is the premise used

\section{The exact finite distribution}\label{sec:law}
In the remaining sections $M=2^N$, $B=M/2$, and $R=R_N$.
Write $C_M(T)=\#\{r:R(r)\le T\}$ and $S_M(T)=M-C_M(T)$.
Thresholds in finite formulas are integers. Set $C_M(0)=0$ and
$C_M(T)=M$ for $T\ge B$; a real threshold is handled by its floor.
The conventions include $N=1$, when both heights are one.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:finite}
For $1\le T\le\lfloor\sqrt M\rfloor$, put
\begin{align}
P(T)&=\sum_{\substack{g\le T\\g\ \odd}}\mu(g)
\lfloor T/g\rfloor\left\lceil\frac{\lfloor T/g\rfloor}{2}\right\rceil,
\label{eq:positivecount}\\
J_M(T)&=\sum_{\substack{b,d\le T\ \odd\\(b,d)=1}}
\pos{\left\lfloor\frac{T-a_0}{b}\right\rfloor
-\left\lceil\frac{M-bT-da_0}{bd}\right\rceil+1},\label{eq:collision}
\end{align}
where $0\le a_0<b$, $a_0d\equiv M\pmod b$, and $a_0=0$ for $b=1$.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:centralCDF}
C_M(T)=1+2P(T)-J_M(T).
\end{equation}
For $T^2\ge M$ and $T<B$, take all even $q$ with $2\le q<M/T$
and all $1\le a<q$ with $(a,q)=1$. Write $v=a^{-1}\bmod q$ in
$\{1,\ldots,q-1\}$, and set
\begin{align}
b_L&=v+q\left\lfloor\frac{T-v}{q}\right\rfloor,&
b_R&=q-v+q\left\lfloor\frac{T-(q-v)}q\right\rfloor,\label{eq:neighbors}\\
\alpha_{a,q}&=\frac{aMb_L-M+qT}{qb_L},&
\beta_{a,q}&=\frac{aMb_R+M-qT}{qb_R}.\label{eq:holeends}
\end{align}
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:highCDF}
S_M(T)=\sum_{\substack{2\le q<M/T\\q\ \mathrm{even}}}
\sum_{\substack{1\le a<q\\(a,q)=1}}
\pos{\lceil\beta_{a,q}\rceil-\lfloor\alpha_{a,q}\rfloor-1}.
\end{equation}
The two ranges cover all integer thresholds and agree at a common
square-root threshold. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mass}
\Pr(R_N=t)=\frac{C_M(t)-C_M(t-1)}M,\qquad 1\le t\le B.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The number of primitive pairs $1\le a,b\le T$, $b$ odd, is $P(T)$
by odd-divisor M\"obius inversion. In each strict numerator-sign half,
the map $(a,b)\mapsto ab^{-1}\bmod M$ is injective: a nonzero
determinant has magnitude at most $TB_T<M$, where $B_T$ is the largest
odd integer at most $T$. If $T^2=M$, $T$ is even and $B_T=T-1$.
The numerator-zero primitive pair $(0,1)$ cannot collide with a
nonzero numerator. A positive/negative collision is exactly
\[
ad+eb=M,\qquad 1\le a,e\le T,\quad b,d\le T\text{ odd}.
\]
The determinant is positive and less than $2M$. Every solution is
automatically primitive and $(b,d)=1$, since a common odd divisor
would divide $M$. Writing $a=a_0+bz$ gives
$e=(M-da_0)/b-dz$. The conditions $1\le a,e\le T$ are precisely
\[
\frac{M-bT-da_0}{bd}\le z\le\frac{T-a_0}b:
\]
the lower bound forces $a>0$ and the upper bound forces $e>0$,
because $M>TB_T$. Each sign half has at most one witness per residue,
so inclusion--exclusion subtracts each collision once. This proves
\eqref{eq:centralCDF}.

For the high range, let $\mathcal F_T^{\odd}$ be the reduced fractions
in $[0,1]$ with odd denominator at most $T$, including $0/1,1/1$.
The exact interval model is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interval}
R_N(r)\le T\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac rM\in\bigcup_{p/b\in\mathcal F_T^{\odd}}
\left[\frac pb-\frac{T}{Mb},\frac pb+\frac{T}{Mb}\right].
\end{equation}
Indeed $a=rb-Mp$ gives $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(p,b)$ because $(b,M)=1$;
division by odd content preserves coverage. The endpoint fractions
provide the wrap intervals $[0,T]$ and $[M-T,M-1]$ on the residue grid.
No interior fraction wraps, since $T<M/2$.

Here are the required Farey facts with their proof. For reduced
$a/q$ with $q\le T$, take the largest $b\le T$ satisfying
$ab\equiv1\pmod q$, and $p=(ab-1)/q$. Then $b>T-q$ and
$ab-pq=1$. A fraction strictly between a determinant-one pair has
positive integer coordinates in that pair, hence denominator at least
$b+q>T$. Thus $p/b$ is the immediate Farey predecessor; the successor
uses $ad\equiv-1\pmod q$. Endpoints follow directly. Consecutive
denominators therefore sum to more than $T$ and have determinant one;
they cannot both be even. Deleting even denominators leaves pairs
that were full Farey neighbors or had exactly one deleted even
fraction $a/q$ between them.

Put $\lambda=T/M$, so $\lambda T\ge1$. Retained full-Farey neighbors
with denominators $b,d$ have distance $1/(bd)$, less than
$\lambda(1/b+1/d)$ because $b+d>T$. Their closed intervals overlap.
Across a deleted even $a/q$, the immediate odd neighbor denominators
are \eqref{eq:neighbors} and exceed $T-q$. If $\lambda q\ge1$ both
intervals reach the center, leaving no hole. Hence only $qT<M$
contributes; such $q$ satisfy $q<M/T\le T$ and occur in the sequence.

When $qT<M$, every retained $p/b$ has determinant distance
$\delta=|ab-pq|\ge1$. Its nearest endpoint on its side is at distance
$(\delta-\lambda q)/(qb)$ from the center. Among determinant-one
candidates, the largest denominator is best. Every $\delta\ge2$
candidate, anywhere in the sequence, is worse, because
\[
\frac{\delta-\lambda q}{b}\ge\frac{2-\lambda q}{T}>\frac1T,
\qquad \frac{1-\lambda q}{b_L},\frac{1-\lambda q}{b_R}<\frac1T.
\]
The latter follows from $T(1-\lambda q)\le T-q<b_L,b_R$.
This global comparison excludes hidden coverage by broad intervals
from more distant centers. The uncovered component is exactly
\[
\left(\frac aq-\frac{1-\lambda q}{qb_L},
\frac aq+\frac{1-\lambda q}{qb_R}\right).
\]
It lies strictly inside its retained Farey gap. These components are
disjoint and exhaust the uncovered set; endpoint gaps are already
covered. Scaling by $M$ gives \eqref{eq:holeends}. An open interval
$(\alpha,\beta)$ contains exactly
$[\lceil\beta\rceil-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor-1]_+$ integers. Endpoints are
covered, and $qT=M$ gives no hole. This proves \eqref{eq:highCDF}.
Both formulas count the original event, so they agree where they
overlap. Equation~\eqref{eq:mass} follows by subtraction.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:finitevariance}
For $j=1,2$, the exact finite logarithmic moments and variance are
\begin{align}
m_{j,N}&=\frac1M\sum_{T=1}^{B-1}S_M(T)
\big[(\log_2(T+1))^j-(\log_2T)^j\big],\label{eq:finitemoments}\\
\operatorname{Var}(\log_2R_N)&=m_{2,N}-m_{1,N}^2.\label{eq:finitevariance}
\end{align}
Every coefficient is given by Theorem~\ref{thm:finite} using finite
integer arithmetic, gcds, inverses, floors and ceilings.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Telescope $(\log_2h)^j$ from height one for each integer $h\in[1,B]$.
An increment at $T$ occurs exactly for residues with $R_N>T$.
Averaging proves \eqref{eq:finitemoments}; the variance identity follows.
For $N=1$ the empty sums give variance zero.
\end{proof}
These are finite arithmetic-logarithmic sums, not a statement that the
variance is rational or has a short elementary formula in $N$.
For $M=4$, the masses $3,1$ at heights $1,2$ give variance $3/16$.
For $M=16$, masses $3,2,6,2,2,1$ at heights $1,2,3,4,5,8$ show
gaps in the support. At $T=4$ the high hole is $(20/3,28/3)$,
containing $7,8,9$; $qT=M$ produces no hole. The extreme heights
at $r=M/2\pm1$, namely $M/4+1$ for $N>2$, are already recorded in
Tian--Qi \cite[Lemma~8, p.~74]{TQ}; the unique top maximum is also
recorded there \cite[Lemma~10, p.~77]{TQ}.

\section{A batched histogram algorithm}\label{sec:algorithm}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:algorithm}
The histogram of $R_N$ and the coefficients of
\eqref{eq:finitemoments} can be assembled in $O(M\log M)=O(N2^N)$
elementary arithmetic operations and $O(M)$ integer storage slots.
Logarithm evaluation to a prescribed precision is separate.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $L=\lfloor\sqrt M\rfloor$, $K=\lceil\sqrt M\rceil$.
The new positive primitive boundary $p_t=P(t)-P(t-1)$ is
\[
p_1=1,\quad p_t=\phi(t)\ (t\ge2\text{ even}),\quad
p_t=3\phi(t)/2\ (t\ge3\text{ odd}).
\]
For even $t$ only the numerator boundary enters, and all coprime
residues modulo $t$ are odd. For odd $t$ the denominator boundary
gives $\phi(t)$; the remaining numerator boundary gives $\phi(t)/2$
by pairing $b$ with $t-b$. The corner is nonprimitive for $t>1$.
A totient sieve supplies these values.

Pass once through the ordered coprime odd pairs $(b,d)\le L$, solving
\eqref{eq:collision} at $L$. Its real solution interval has length
\[
1-\frac{(L-b)(L-d)+(M-L^2)}{bd}<1;
\]
strictness at a square uses $b,d\le L-1$. There is at most one
integer solution $(a,e)$ per pair. Put its event at
$\tau=\max(a,e,b,d)$ and let $j_t$ count such events. The central
masses are $\mathbf1_{t=1}+2p_t-j_t$, $1\le t\le L$: a collision
is subtracted exactly when both witnesses have become available.
For $N=1,2$, $L=B$ and this is the entire histogram.

For $N\ge3$, $K<B$. Construct \eqref{eq:highCDF}'s open holes once,
at $T=K$. Every survivor $R_N(r)>K$ has one hole about a reduced
even fraction $a/q$. Put $d=|aM-qr|$, and let $u$ be the least
positive residue of $a^{-1}\bmod q$ on the left (including equality),
or $-a^{-1}\bmod q$ on the right. It is odd. With its side neighbor
$b_0$ at $K$, membership gives
\[
db_0<M-qK,\qquad b_0>K-q,\qquad qK<M.
\]
Hence $q<K$, $d<K$ (if $d>0$, use $(M-qK)/(K-q)\le K$),
$d^2<M$, and $x=M/(q+d)>b_0\ge u$.
Positive consecutive members of $u+q\Z$ straddling $x$ are
\[
b_-=u+q\left\lfloor\frac{M-u(q+d)}{q(q+d)}\right\rfloor,
\qquad b_+=b_-+q.
\]
Each gives an integral determinant-one witness of height
$f(b)=\max(b,|M-db|/q)$, since $bd\equiv M\pmod q$.
Below $x$ the function is $(M-db)/q$, decreasing or constant;
above $x$ it equals $b$ up to $M/d$, after which both branches
increase. Also $f(b_+)=b_+$: for $d>q$ use
\[
(d-q)(x+q)-M=\frac{q(d^2-q^2-2M)}{d+q}<0;
\]
for $d\le q$ this is immediate. Thus the progression minimum is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twocandidate}
h=\min\{(M-db_-)/q,b_+\}.
\end{equation}
These witnesses imply $K<h\le f(b_0)\le M/q\le B$.
For any integer $K\le T<h$, $qT<M$ and $T<b_+$, so the largest
side denominator at threshold $T$ is at most $b_-$. Its numerator
cost exceeds $T$. The residue remains inside this cusp's open hole;
the global comparison in Theorem~\ref{thm:finite}'s proof excludes
all other fractions. Hence $R_N(r)=h$. For $d=0$ this gives $M/q$.

Insert each survivor at \eqref{eq:twocandidate}. If $K=L+1$, insert
the bridge mass $M-S_M(K)-C_M(L)$ at $K$; if $K=L$ the central
part already contains it. There is no skipped integer between $L$
and $K$. Central residues, bridge and survivors exhaust $M$.
There are $O(M)$ central pairs and initial even fractions.
Gcds and inverses use $O(\log M)$ Euclidean arithmetic steps each.
Cache the inverse once per cusp; at most $M$ surviving integers in
disjoint holes then need constant arithmetic work each. Dense bins
and cumulative sums need $O(M)$ additions and storage slots.
\end{proof}
This cost is exponential in $N$ and is not a bit-complexity theorem or
a timing comparison with all alternatives. The companion's batched
route uses no defining minimum oracle or all-threshold rescan.

\section{The marked lattice limit}\label{sec:bridge}
We prove the discrete-to-continuous bridge explicitly. All vectors are
rows. Set
\[
G=\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R),\quad
\Delta=\Gamma^0(2)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}
\in\mathrm{SL}_2(\Z):b\equiv0\pmod2\right\},\quad X=\Delta\backslash G,
\]
\[
\mathcal A=\{(k,b)\in\Z^2:b\text{ odd}\},\quad
\mathcal A_{\rm pr}=\{w\in\mathcal A:\gcd(w_1,w_2)=1\}.
\]
\[
h(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-x&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
a(Q)=\operatorname{diag}(Q,Q^{-1}).
\]
The stabilizer of $\mathcal A$ and of its primitive subset is
$\Delta$. The last coordinate of $(k,b)\gamma$ is
$k\gamma_{12}+b\gamma_{22}$; preservation of both primitive parity
classes forces $\gamma_{12}=0$, $\gamma_{22}=1$ modulo two, and these
conditions suffice. Reduction modulo two shows index three.
Use Haar probability $\mu_\Delta$ on $X$. The horocycle period is
one, since $h(x)\in\Delta$ exactly for integral $x$.

The external inputs are Heersink's continuous horosphere theorem
\cite[\S2, pp.~3--4; Theorem~4, p.~7]{Heersink}, valid for every
finite-index subgroup, and Howe--Moore
\cite[Theorem~1.1, p.~2; Definition~2.6, p.~4]{Ciobotaru}.
The first, in dimension one with source torus $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$
and both measures of mass one, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous}
\int_0^1f(\Delta h(x)a(Q))dx\longrightarrow\int_Xf\,d\mu_\Delta
\end{equation}
for bounded continuous $f$; its joint form retains the source coordinate.
The second applies to connected noncompact simple real Lie groups
with finite center. Matrix coefficients of a strongly continuous
unitary representation without invariant vectors vanish at infinity.
No discrete marked theorem is imported. The following smearing argument
uses the method of Marklof
\cite[\S4, Proposition~9, equations~(4.8)--(4.13)]{MarklofSmall}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:discrete}
For $g_{M,r}=h(r/M)a(\sqrt M)$,
$\nu_M=M^{-1}\sum_{r=0}^{M-1}\delta_{\Delta g_{M,r}}$ converges weakly
to $\mu_\Delta$ as $N\to\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Conjugation gives $g_{M,r}h(1)=g_{M,r+1}$, with the last point
identified with the first. Thus $\nu_M$ is exactly invariant under
$T:z\mapsto zh(1)$. Smearing gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smearing}
\bar\nu_M:=\int_0^1\nu_M\cdot h(s)ds
=\int_0^1\delta_{\Delta h(x)a(\sqrt M)}dx,
\end{equation}
by $x=(r+s)/M$. Thus $\bar\nu_M\Rightarrow\mu_\Delta$.
Choose a compact $C$ of smeared mass at least $1-\varepsilon$ for
all sufficiently large levels, and put
$C'=\{zh(-s):z\in C,0\le s\le1\}$. This is compact and
$\bar\nu_M(C)\le\nu_M(C')$. Adding the finitely many remaining
supports proves tightness. The locally compact metrizable space $X$
therefore admits subsequential probability limits.

Since $-I\in\Delta$, the action factors through
$\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$, satisfying the Howe--Moore hypotheses.
The regular representation on $L^2_0(X,\mu_\Delta)$ is unitary and
strongly continuous, with no invariant vectors: invariance under the
transitive $G$ action forces a function to be constant almost everywhere.
The elements $h(n)$ escape compact subsets; Howe--Moore gives mixing
of $T$ for Haar probability, hence ergodicity.

Let $\nu$ be a subsequential limit. Exact invariance passes to it.
For bounded continuous tests, dominated convergence in $s$ gives
\[
\mu_\Delta=\int_0^1\nu\cdot h(s)ds.
\]
Each measure in this barycenter is $T$-invariant since $h(s)$ commutes
with $h(1)$. Ergodic probabilities are extreme among invariant
probabilities, so $\nu\cdot h(s)=\mu_\Delta$ almost everywhere.
A countable determining family of tests gives a common full-measure
set of such $s$. Continuity of the test integrals in $s$ extends
equality to $s=0$. Therefore $\nu=\mu_\Delta$; tightness and uniqueness
prove convergence.
\end{proof}

Let $B_t=[-t,t]\times(0,t]$ and let $Z(g)$ be the least
$\max(|u|,v)$ among $(u,v)\in\mathcal A_{\rm pr}g$, $v>0$.
It is positive, finite and attained. Discreteness gives positivity
and attainment. For finiteness the coset $(0,1)g+2\Z^2g$ has finite
covering radius, whereas the sector $v>0,|u|<v/2$ contains arbitrarily
large disks. A coset point there has odd coefficient denominator;
division by its odd content gives a primitive point in some $B_t$.
The void set $W_t=\{\Delta g:\mathcal A_{\rm pr}g\cap B_t=\varnothing\}$
is a Haar continuity set. Locally bounded inverses restrict possible
hits in the compact closure of $B_t$ to finitely many integer rows,
none approaching zero. Discontinuity requires an image on a face,
including $v=0$. For a fixed nonzero row $w$, the map $g\mapsto wg$
is a submersion onto $\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}$. Preimages of
planar-null faces are Haar-null; countable union and passage to the
discrete quotient preserve nullity.
Finally,
\[
(k,b)g_{M,r}=((Mk-rb)/\sqrt M,b/\sqrt M).
\]
With $a=rb-Mk$ this identifies the box event exactly with
$R_N\le t\sqrt M$. The equality $\gcd(k,b)=\gcd(a,b)$ and odd-content
division preserve the congruence and mark. Lemma~\ref{lem:discrete}
and the continuity-set theorem yield
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weakR}
Z_N:=R_N/\sqrt M\ \Rightarrow\ Z,\qquad
\Pr(Z>t)=\mu_\Delta(W_t).
\end{equation}

\section{Identification with the published Boca--Gologan law}\label{sec:BG}
The geometric law is already published. We identify its model and
scale; its authors did not assert a finite binary-string theorem.
Set
\[
U=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\mathcal S=\{(q,a)\in\Z^2:q-a\text{ odd}\},\quad H=U^{-1}\Delta U.
\]
Since $(k,b)U=(k-b,k)$, $\mathcal A U=\mathcal S$. The map
$\Delta g\mapsto H U^{-1}g$ preserves Haar probability and the
marked void events. For
$J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and
$D_t=\operatorname{diag}(t,t^{-1})$,
\[
B_tJD_t=(0,t^2]\times[-1,1]=:C_{t^2}.
\]
Right Haar invariance gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:squarelaw}
\Pr(Z>t)=\mu_H\{Hg:\mathcal Sg\cap C_{t^2}=\varnothing\}.
\end{equation}
Primitivity can be omitted: marked vectors have odd content, and
division contracts these origin-centered windows.

For slopes $u\in[0,1]$, put
\[
g_\epsilon(u)=\begin{pmatrix}1&-u\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}).
\]
A row $(q,a)$ maps to $(\epsilon q,(a-qu)/\epsilon)$.
The void event in $C_\lambda$ is survival past horizontal distance
$\lambda/\epsilon$ against vertical segments of half-length $\epsilon$
at $(q,a)$, $q-a$ odd, starting at the omitted origin. This is BG's
modulus-two model \cite[Theorem~1.1(i), p.~1045; definition, p.~1044]{BG}.
Its parameter is $\epsilon q$, not $2\epsilon q$.

We identify BG's angular average with Haar. Work first on
$\Gamma(2)\backslash G$, whose horocycle period is two.
$\Gamma(2)\subset H$ and $[H:\Gamma(2)]=2$; projection preserves
probability Haar. Conjugation by $J$ normalizes $\Gamma(2)$ and
\[
J^{-1}h(x)a(\epsilon^{-1})J
=\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}).
\]
Use the two-periodic weight
$w(x)=8\mathbf1_{[-1,0]}(x)/(\pi(1+x^2))$, of mass one for $dx/2$.
The joint Heersink theorem with this finite-index subgroup and $x=-u$
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:angularHaar}
\lim_{\epsilon\to0}\frac4\pi\int_0^1
\mathbf1_{\mathcal Sg_\epsilon(u)\cap C_\lambda=\varnothing}
\frac{du}{1+u^2}
=\mu_H\{\mathcal Sg\cap C_\lambda=\varnothing\}.
\end{equation}
The bounded weight is $L^1$-approximable; the void boundary is
Haar-null by the submersion argument. Joint continuous-test
equidistribution therefore extends to this event and weight. We do
not assume equidistribution on an unweighted half-period.

BG's theorem gives, for intervals $I\subset[0,1]$ of length comparable
to $\epsilon^c$, angular survival
$c_I G_2(\lambda)+O_{\delta,\lambda,2}(\epsilon^{\theta-\delta})$,
where $c_I=\int_Idu/(1+u^2)$ and
$\theta=\min(c+c_1,1/2-2c_1)$. Partition into
$\lceil\epsilon^{-1/8}\rceil$ equal cells; choose $c=c_1=1/8$ and
$0<\delta<1/8$. The uniform interval error sums to
$O(\epsilon^{1/8-\delta})\to0$, and $\sum c_I=\pi/4$.
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BGlaw}
\boxed{\Pr(Z>t)=G_2(t^2).}
\end{equation}
This fixed-$\lambda$ source estimate identifies the law and gives no
rate for the dyadic sample.

The specialization of the published survival is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:G2}
G_2(x)=\begin{cases}
1-8x/\pi^2,&0<x\le1/2,\\
1-6x/\pi^2+2H_2(x)/\pi^2,&1/2\le x\le1,\\
4H_3(x)/\pi^2,&x\ge1,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{align*}
H_2(x)&=3x-2+\zeta(2)-(\ln x)^2
+2(1-x)\ln(1/x-1)-2\operatorname{Li}_2(x),\\
H_3(x)&=\operatorname{Li}_2(1/x)-(x-1)\ln(1-1/x)-1.
\end{align*}
Products at singular endpoints are interpreted continuously.
Differentiation gives the density of $\Lambda=Z^2$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:density}
g_2(x)=\begin{cases}
8/\pi^2,&0<x<1/2,\\
\dfrac4{\pi^2x}\left[1-(1-x)\ln\dfrac{1-x}{x}\right],&1/2<x<1,\\
\dfrac4{\pi^2x}\left[1+(x-1)\ln(1-1/x)\right],&x>1.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The tail sign follows by differentiating $H_3$; the opposite sign in
BG's density display on p.~1069 is a typographical error. We import
the survival and derive the density.

\section{Logarithmic moments and limiting variance}\label{sec:moments}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:tails}
Uniformly in $N$, for $z>0$,
\[
\Pr(Z_N\le z)\le\min(1,3z^2),\qquad
\Pr(Z_N>z)\le\min(1,4z^{-2}).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $X\ge1$, the number of pairs with $|a|\le X$, odd $1\le b\le X$
is at most $(2\lfloor X\rfloor+1)\lceil\lfloor X\rfloor/2\rceil
\le3X^2$. Each pair determines one residue. Below one the event is empty.

For the upper tail let $\lambda(r)$ be the shortest Euclidean vector
length in the full congruence lattice $\Lambda_r$. A complete
Gauss-reduced basis $u,v$ satisfies
$\|u\|\le\|v\|$, $2|u\cdot v|\le\|u\|^2$.
First order a basis so that $\|u\|\le\|v\|$. If the scalar-product
inequality fails, replace $v$ by $v-ku$, where $k$ is a nearest integer
to $(u\cdot v)/\|u\|^2$, and reorder. Each such subtraction strictly
decreases the positive integer sum of squared norms; swaps leave it
unchanged. Thus the process terminates at the two displayed inequalities.
They imply that $u$ is shortest. For $ku+tv$ the
cases $t=0$, $|t|=1$ follow from the scalar-product bound; for
$|t|\ge2$ the perpendicular component has length at least
$2M/\|u\|\ge\|u\|$. Indeed the reduced angle has sine at least
$\sqrt3/2$, so $\|u\|\|v\|\le2M/\sqrt3$.
Thus $\|u\|=\lambda(r)$. The denominators of the basis generate
$\Z$, so one is odd and its height is at most $\|v\|$. Hence
$R_N(r)\le2M/(\sqrt3\lambda(r))$.

For $1\le Y<M$, a nonzero vector of length at most $Y$ cannot be
horizontal; orient its denominator $1\le b\le\lfloor Y\rfloor$.
For $g=\gcd(b,M)$ the congruence has
$g(2\lfloor Y/g\rfloor+1)$ incidences with residues. Summing gives
at most $3Y\lfloor Y\rfloor\le3Y^2$. This includes even denominators
and nonprimitive vectors. If $R_N>X$, then
$\lambda(r)<2M/(\sqrt3X)$. When $4M/X^2<1$ this cutoff is less
than $M$; below one the event is empty, otherwise the incidence bound
gives at most $4M^2/X^2$ residues. In the remaining range the
probability bound one suffices. Set $X=z\sqrt M$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:limit}
Put $\ell=\ln2$, $L_4=\operatorname{Li}_4(1/2)$ and
\begin{align*}
Q={}&4\zeta(4)+3\zeta(2)-\tfrac74\zeta(3)
+\tfrac12\zeta(2)\ell^2-\tfrac74\ell\zeta(3)-\tfrac1{12}\ell^4-2L_4,\\
\eta_1={}&\frac{7\zeta(3)}{2\pi^2}-1,\qquad \eta_2=\frac4{\pi^2}Q.
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align}
\E\log_2R_N&=N/2+c_R+o(1),&c_R&=\frac{\eta_1}{2\ell},\label{eq:asymmean}\\
\operatorname{Var}(\log_2R_N)&\longrightarrow\sigma_R^2,&
\sigma_R^2&=\frac{\eta_2-\eta_1^2}{4\ell^2}.\label{eq:asymvariance}
\end{align}
Exact rational enclosures are
\begin{align*}
-0.413852654438430019863271&\le c_R\le-0.413852654438430019863270,\\
0.892302571204274625294755&\le\sigma_R^2\le0.892302571204274625294756.
\end{align*}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We evaluate $\eta_j=\int_0^\infty(\ln x)^jg_2(x)dx$.
For $h=1/2$ define
\[
A_j=\int_h^1(\ln x)^j\ln(1-x)\frac{dx}x,\quad
B_j=\int_h^1(\ln x)^j\ln(1-x)dx,
\]
\[
E_j=\int_h^1(\ln x)^j\frac{dx}x=\frac{(-1)^j\ell^{j+1}}{j+1},
\qquad D_j=\int_h^1(\ln x)^jdx.
\]
The middle contribution, multiplied by $\pi^2/4$, is
$M_j=E_j-A_j+B_j+E_{j+1}-D_{j+1}$.
Directly differentiable primitives for $A_0,A_1,A_2$ are
\[
-\operatorname{Li}_2(x),\quad
-\ln x\operatorname{Li}_2(x)+\operatorname{Li}_3(x),\quad
-(\ln x)^2\operatorname{Li}_2(x)+2\ln x\operatorname{Li}_3(x)
-2\operatorname{Li}_4(x).
\]
With $L_j=\operatorname{Li}_j(1/2)$ this gives
\[
A_0=-\zeta(2)+L_2,\quad A_1=\zeta(3)-\ell L_2-L_3,
\quad A_2=-2\zeta(4)+\ell^2L_2+2\ell L_3+2L_4.
\]
Integration by parts with the primitive
$V(x)=(1-x)(1-\ln(1-x))$ of $\ln(1-x)$ gives
\begin{align*}
B_0&=-(\ell+1)/2,\\
B_1&=1+\ell^2/2+A_0,\\
B_2&=-\ell^2(1+\ell)/2-2E_1+2D_1+2A_1-2B_1.
\end{align*}
The elementary values needed are
$D_1=(\ell-1)/2$, $D_2=1-\ell-\ell^2/2$ and
$D_3=-3+3\ell+3\ell^2/2+\ell^3/2$.

The half-values in the simplification are
\[
L_2=\zeta(2)/2-\ell^2/2,\qquad
L_3=7\zeta(3)/8-\zeta(2)\ell/2+\ell^3/6.
\]
Dilogarithm reflection
$\operatorname{Li}_2(x)+\operatorname{Li}_2(1-x)
=\zeta(2)-\ln x\ln(1-x)$ follows by differentiation and $x\to0$.
The same argument gives
$\operatorname{Li}_2(-x/(1-x))=-\operatorname{Li}_2(x)-\ln^2(1-x)/2$.
These identities verify the derivative of
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{Li}_3(x)+\operatorname{Li}_3(-x/(1-x))
+\operatorname{Li}_3(1-x)
={}&\zeta(3)+\zeta(2)\ln(1-x)\\
&-\tfrac12\ln x\ln^2(1-x)+\tfrac16\ln^3(1-x).
\end{align*}
Both sides tend to $\zeta(3)$ at zero. At $x=1/2$,
$\operatorname{Li}_3(-1)=-3\zeta(3)/4$ from its absolutely convergent
alternating series, which proves the $L_3$ value.
Substitution now yields
\begin{align*}
M_0&=\zeta(2)/2,\\
M_1&=\ell-\zeta(2)/2-\zeta(3)/8,\\
M_2&=2\zeta(4)+\zeta(2)+\zeta(3)/4-2\ell-\ell^2
+\zeta(2)\ell^2/2\\
&\hspace{12mm}-7\ell\zeta(3)/4-\ell^4/12-2L_4.
\end{align*}
On $(0,1/2)$ the normalized contributions are
$1,-\ell-1,\ell^2+2\ell+2$ for $j=0,1,2$.
On $x>1$ the density has the positive expansion
\[
g_2(x)=\frac4{\pi^2}\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{x^{-n-1}}{n(n+1)}.
\]
Tonelli and $\int_1^\infty(\ln x)^jx^{-n-1}dx=j!/n^{j+1}$ give
normalized tail contributions $\zeta(2)-1$,
$\zeta(3)-\zeta(2)+1$, and $2(\zeta(4)-\zeta(3)+\zeta(2)-1)$.
Adding and multiplying by $4/\pi^2$ proves $\eta_0=1$ and the
stated $\eta_1,\eta_2$. Log powers are absolutely integrable at zero;
the positive expansion justifies their tail integrals. No quadrature
is needed.

For transfer let $Y_N=\ln Z_N$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:tails} gives $\Pr(|Y_N|>T)\le7e^{-2T}$ for $T\ge0$.
Layer cake, including the boundary term, gives
\[
\E[Y_N^2\mathbf1_{|Y_N|>T}]
\le7(T^2+T+1/2)e^{-2T}\longrightarrow0
\]
uniformly in $N$. Thus \eqref{eq:weakR} transfers both log moments:
$\E Y_N\to\eta_1/2$, $\E Y_N^2\to\eta_2/4$, since $\Lambda=Z^2$.
As $\log_2R_N=N/2+Y_N/\ell$, subtracting the squared mean proves
\eqref{eq:asymmean}--\eqref{eq:asymvariance}. The square parameter
accounts for the factor four in the variance denominator.

For the enclosures use Euler-transformed eta, $p=2,3,4$:
\[
(1-2^{1-p})\zeta(p)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{\Delta^ka_0}{2^{k+1}},
\quad a_n=(n+1)^{-p},\quad\Delta a_n=a_n-a_{n+1}.
\]
Indeed $\Delta^ka_0=\frac1{(p-1)!}\int_0^1(1-x)^k(-\ln x)^{p-1}dx$
lies in $(0,1]$; the positive geometric sum gives the eta integral
with factor $1/(1+x)$. Its tail after $96$ terms is in $[0,2^{-96}]$.
Use $40$ terms of $\ell=2\sum_{j\ge0}3^{-2j-1}/(2j+1)$ with
remainder at most $9/[4\cdot81\cdot3^{81}]$, and $96$ terms of
$L_4=\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}/k^4$ with remainder at most $2^{-96}/97^4$.
Set $\pi^2=6\zeta(2)$ exactly. Finite differences and interval
operations are rational; products use all four endpoint products,
squares handle zero when necessary and divisors exclude zero.
Floor/ceiling rounding to $10^{24}$ gives the displayed endpoints,
reproduced by \path{companion/limit_constants.py}. This certifies
evaluation of the expression conditional on the analytic theorem
and its named external imports.
\end{proof}

\section{The raw mean and the Jensen gap}\label{sec:raw}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:raw}
For $c_{\rm raw}=32\ln(1+\sqrt2)/(3\pi^2)$,
\[
\E R_N\sim c_{\rm raw}2^{N/2},\qquad
\log_2\E R_N-\E\log_2R_N\longrightarrow\log_2c_{\rm raw}-c_R>0.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Including the boundary term, Lemma~\ref{lem:tails} gives
\[
\E[Z_N\mathbf1_{Z_N>K}]
=K\Pr(Z_N>K)+\int_K^\infty\Pr(Z_N>t)dt\le8/K.
\]
Thus $\E Z_N\to\E Z$. From \eqref{eq:density}, the three contributions
to $(\pi^2/4)\E Z$ are
\[
A=\sqrt2/3,\quad B=\int_{1/2}^1x^{-1/2}[1-(1-x)\ln((1-x)/x)]dx,
\]
\[
C=\int_1^\infty x^{-1/2}[1+(x-1)\ln(1-1/x)]dx.
\]
For $B$ substitute $y=\sqrt x$. A directly differentiable primitive is
\[
F(y)=\frac{2y}3-2\left(y-\frac{y^3}3\right)
\ln\frac{1-y^2}{y^2}-\frac83\operatorname{atanh}y.
\]
The singular terms cancel at one:
$F(1)=2/3-(8/3)\ln2$. For $a=1/\sqrt2$,
$F(a)=2a/3-(8/3)\operatorname{atanh}a$.
Positive tail expansion and Tonelli give
\[
C=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n(n+1)(n-1/2)}=\frac83\ln2-\frac23.
\]
For this equality sum the partial fractions
$-2/n+2/[3(n+1)]+4/[3(n-1/2)]$ with a common finite cutoff, using
$\sum_{n=1}^K(n-1/2)^{-1}=2H_{2K}-H_K$.
Since $A=2a/3$, the sum is $(8/3)\operatorname{atanh}(1/\sqrt2)
=(8/3)\ln(1+\sqrt2)$, proving the coefficient.
Combine with \eqref{eq:asymmean} for the gap. Strict Jensen applies
to the nonconstant limiting law, whose positive mean and integrable
logarithm have been proved. There is no positive gap bound at every
finite length: $N=1$ has gap zero.
\end{proof}

\section{Directed logarithms using integer arithmetic}\label{app:logs}
We specify the rounding used in \eqref{eq:bridge}.
For a rational $v=u/w$ with $1\le v\le2$ let
$z=(u-w)/(u+w)\in[0,1/3]$. Then
\[
 \ln v=2\sum_{j=0}^{11}\frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1}
              +2\sum_{j=12}^\infty\frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1}.
\]
To compute an upper, replace $Sz$ by its ceiling, compute its square
divided by $S$ with upward rounding, start the scaled power with that
ceiling, and at each step round both division by $2j+1$ and the
multiplication by the scaled square divided by $S$ upwards.
Twice the twelve-term sum is an upper for the partial sum.
The exact tail, using $z\le1/2$, is bounded by
\[
 \frac{2z^{25}}{25(1-z^2)}
                  \le\frac8{3\cdot25\cdot2^{25}}.
\]
Add the integer ceiling of $8S/(3\cdot25\cdot2^{25})$.
For a lower, use downward rounding at every step and omit the
nonnegative remainder.
For $v=1$, return exactly zero.
These rules give a lower $\ell^-_2$ and upper $\ell^+_2$ for $S\ln2$.

For an arbitrary rational $x=u/w\ge1$, find the unique integer $k\ge0$
with $2^kw\le u<2^{k+1}w$, by integer bit lengths and a comparison.
Apply the unit-interval upper to $u/(2^kw)$ and add $k\ell^+_2$.
Every operation is an integer comparison, product, floor or ceiling.
Since all rounding maps are monotone on nonnegative inputs, the computed
scaled powers bound the true scaled powers by induction.
The uniform tail bounds the true analytic remainder; it does not assume
that an upward-rounded approximation of $z$ still equals $z$.
This proves the directed inequalities used by every summand of the
finite bridge.
